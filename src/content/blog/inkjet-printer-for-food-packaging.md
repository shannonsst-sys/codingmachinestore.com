Food packaging is one of the most demanding jobs for a coding machine. The print has to be legible on everything from a paper carton to a glossy plastic pouch, it has to survive transport and refrigeration, and it has to be applied cleanly — no smell, no mess, no ink where it should not be.

This guide covers what to look for — and what to avoid — when choosing an inkjet printer for food packaging.

## First, print on the packaging, not the food

A thermal inkjet (TIJ) printer codes the outside of the pack: the carton, the pouch, the label, the tray. It does not print directly on food, and it should not. The code — expiry date, batch number, production date — goes on the outer packaging, where it is visible to the retailer and the consumer.

That distinction matters when you choose ink. Standard TIJ inks are made for packaging surfaces, not for direct food contact. If your product needs a code printed directly onto an edible surface, that is a specialist job with food-grade inks and its own regulations — talk to your supplier before you buy anything, and check what your market's food-safety rules require.

## Match the ink to the packaging surface

Food comes in many packs, and each surface takes ink differently:

- **Paper cartons, kraft boxes and paper bags** are porous: the fibres absorb ink, so a water-based TIJ ink works well and dries into the surface. This is the cheapest ink option and the cleanest to handle.
- **Plastic pouches, film, foil and coated cartons** are non-porous: water-based ink beads up and smears. Use a solvent quick-dry ink, which dries in seconds on plastic film, laminated pouches and glossy board.
- **Glass jars and plastic bottles** need solvent ink too — and a quick adhesion test before you commit, because coatings and condensation change how ink sticks.

Our TIJ printers use 42 ml cartridges in 12.7 mm and 25.4 mm widths, with water-based, solvent quick-dry and oil-based inks, so the same printer can switch ink types when your packaging changes. A 12.7 mm cartridge costs from $30 and a 25.4 mm cartridge from $50. Every inkjet printer we sell includes one cartridge per print head, so you can test on your real packaging from day one.

## Clean operation matters more in food than anywhere else

A food packing area has to stay clean, and your coder should help, not fight you:

- **TIJ printers have no ink bottles, no messy refills and no solvent smell in the room** when you use water-based ink on paper packaging. The cartridge is sealed — you swap it in seconds and keep packing.
- **Solvent inks dry fast but have an odour**, so if you pack open food nearby, keep the coding station away from the open product or use water-based ink on paper packs.
- **Hot ribbon coders** are another clean option for flexible food packaging such as pouches and bags: they print with a heated ribbon instead of liquid ink, so there is nothing to spill or dry out — but text only, no barcodes. Our guide to [hot ribbon coders vs inkjet printers](/blog/hot-ribbon-coder-vs-inkjet-printer/) compares the running costs. **Ink roller coders** are the same idea for paper bags and cartons, printing fixed text with a solid ink roll.

Avoid any coder that needs open ink, regular cleaning with solvents, or a warm-up that holds up your packing line.

## Match the machine to your volume

**Small batches, a few hundred packs a day.** A [handheld TIJ printer](/products/a5-handheld-inkjet-printer/) is the cheapest way to start. The M70 mini handheld starts at $180 and the A5 at $210. You hold it against the pack and pull the trigger — no conveyor, no setup — and it codes cartons, pouches, trays and large items that never move.

**A packing line, hundreds or thousands of packs a day.** An [online TIJ printer](/category/inkjet-packaging-line/) mounts over the belt and prints as packs pass underneath. The T6 online printer at $230 is the usual starting point; a sensor triggers the print, so every pack gets the same code in the same place without anyone touching it.

**Trays, cartons and flat packs at a bench.** A [desktop inkjet printer](/products/k90-desktop-inkjet-printer/) like the K90 at $250 sits on the bench and codes packs as you feed them past the head — useful when you pack in one place and the product comes to the printer.

**Eggs and trays.** Egg coding is a special case: the 6-head egg inkjet printer codes whole trays at once, with one cartridge per head. If you pack eggs, look for a machine built for that job rather than adapting a handheld.

## What the print has to carry

Food packaging codes usually need three things: the expiry or best-before date, the batch or lot number, and sometimes a production line code. Some buyers also need a barcode or QR code for traceability — and if you do, you need an inkjet printer, because ribbon and ink-roller coders cannot print scannable codes.

One 12.7 mm cartridge prints one line up to 12.7 mm high — enough for a date and a batch code in one line. If you need two lines, use a two-head printer or a 25.4 mm cartridge. TIJ prints at 300–600 dpi, sharp enough for small text and scannable barcodes, but only if you give the characters room: keep the message short and leave a clear zone around the code, with no artwork underneath it.

## What to avoid

- **The wrong ink for the surface.** Water-based ink on a plastic pouch smears; solvent ink on a paper carton is wasted money. Test on your real packaging first.
- **Printing over a crease, seam or tape.** The printhead sits only 2–5 mm from the surface, so a bump or a fold blurs the print. Code on a flat, smooth panel.
- **A code that rubs off in the fridge or the truck.** Cold, damp and handling all test the print. Rub the sample after 10 seconds, then after an hour in the fridge, and stack and tape over it before you commit.
- **Buying on machine price alone.** The cartridge is the running cost. A cheap printer with an expensive cartridge costs more over a year than a slightly dearer printer with cheap cartridges. Our [inkjet printer cost guide](/blog/how-much-does-an-inkjet-printer-cost/) breaks down the full math.

## Test on your real packaging before you buy

Before you run a full shift with a new coder, print a sample on the exact pack you ship — not a clean offcut. Coatings, recycled content and condensation change how ink behaves. Read it from a metre away, rub it, refrigerate it, and scan it if it includes a barcode. A code that looks fine but does not scan is worse than no code at all.
