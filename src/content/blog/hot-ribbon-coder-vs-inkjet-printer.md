If you pack products in plastic bags, film or foil pouches, you need a date or batch code on every pack — and two very different machines can do the job: a hot ribbon coder or an inkjet printer. One costs as little as $47, the other prints barcodes and QR codes. Which one actually costs less for your job?

This guide compares the two on machine price, cost per print, print quality and flexibility, so you can pick the right coder for flexible packaging.

## How a hot ribbon coder works

A hot ribbon coder is a contact printer. Heated metal type presses a hot-stamping ribbon against the packaging film, and the heat transfers ink from the ribbon onto the surface. The type characters are physical pieces — copper or steel characters you arrange by hand in a type holder — so the message is fixed until you change the characters.

That sounds old-fashioned, and in a way it is. But on flexible packaging it works beautifully. The heat bonds the print to plastic film, laminated pouches, foil and coated bags, giving a clean, smudge-resistant mark that dries instantly — there is no wet ink to smear. Ribbon coders print dates, batch and lot numbers, and a line or two of fixed text, typically in one colour (black is standard; coloured ribbons are available).

## How an inkjet printer works

An inkjet printer is a non-contact printer. Our inkjet range uses TIJ (thermal inkjet): tiny heaters in the cartridge fire drops of ink onto the product from a few millimetres away. The cartridge is the printhead — when it runs out, you swap it and have a brand-new head, with no cleaning and no service visit.

The big difference is what it can print. Inkjet handles variable data: dates that change every day, batch codes, counters, logos, barcodes and QR codes, at 300–600 dpi. A [handheld TIJ printer](/category/inkjet-standalone/) like the A5 at $210 codes cartons, bags and pallets by hand, and an [online TIJ printer](/category/inkjet-packaging-line/) like the T6 at $230 mounts over a conveyor. ## Which prints cheaper?

If your message rarely changes — the same product, a date and a batch code, thousands of identical packs — the ribbon coder wins on cost. The machine is cheaper, and the consumable is a ribbon roll from $0.80, so the cost per pack is a fraction of a cent. A roll lasts a long time because each impression uses only a short strip of ribbon.

If your message changes often — different products, different dates, a barcode or QR code per pack — the inkjet printer wins on time. Changing a ribbon coder's type characters by hand takes minutes and stops the line; changing an inkjet message takes seconds on a touchscreen. And a ribbon coder simply cannot print a barcode or QR code, so if your customer or retailer requires one, inkjet is the only option of the two.

Think of it as fixed vs variable. Fixed message, high volume, film packaging: ribbon. Variable message, codes that must scan, mixed products: inkjet.

## Which surfaces decide the choice?

Ribbon coders are at their best on flexible packaging — the thin films, foils and laminated pouches that an inkjet head can struggle to wet. The heat transfer bonds well to these surfaces and the print resists rubbing.

But ribbon coders are poor on plain corrugated cartons and rough paper: the heat and pressure need a smooth, heat-sealable surface. If your main product is a carton, an inkjet printer or an [ink roller coder](/category/ink-wheel-coders/) is the better tool — ink rollers like the HP-280 at $232.90 print well on paper bags and cartons.

Inkjet, meanwhile, works on almost anything once you match the ink: water-based ink for porous cartons and paper, solvent quick-dry ink for plastic film and coated surfaces. If you print on both cartons and bags, one inkjet printer with the right cartridges covers both jobs.

## Which one should you choose?

**Choose a hot ribbon coder if:**

- You print the same date and batch code on thousands of identical bags or pouches.
- Your packaging is plastic film, foil or laminated pouches.
- You want the lowest possible cost per print and the cheapest machine.
- You never need barcodes, QR codes or logos on the pack.

**Choose an inkjet printer if:**

- Your message changes every job — different products, dates or batch codes.
- You need barcodes or QR codes that a scanner or phone must read.
- You print on cartons and bags as well as film, and want one machine for all of them.
- You want to change the message in seconds, not by swapping type characters.
