Every carton that leaves your warehouse needs an expiry date — and a smudged, faint or missing date can mean a rejected shipment or a retailer refusing the stock. Printing expiry dates on cartons is not hard, but the wrong machine or the wrong ink turns a simple job into daily rework.

This guide walks through the five decisions that decide whether your date codes come out clean, every time: the machine, the ink, the print size, the position, and the test.

## Step 1: Match the machine to your volume

**A few cartons a day, by hand.** A [handheld TIJ printer](/products/a5-handheld-inkjet-printer/) is the cheapest way to start. You hold it against the carton, pull the trigger, and the date prints. The A3 handheld starts at $193 and the A5 at $210. No setup, no conveyor, and you can code cartons, pallets and large items that never move.

**A conveyor line, hundreds of cartons a day.** An [online TIJ printer](/category/inkjet-packaging-line/) mounts over the belt and prints as cartons pass underneath. The T6 online printer at $230 is the usual starting point; a sensor triggers the print, so every carton gets the same code in the same place without anyone touching it.

**A packing station, cartons stacked in one place.** A [desktop inkjet printer](/products/k90-desktop-inkjet-printer/) like the K90 at $250 sits on the bench and codes cartons as you feed them past the head. It suits small workshops where cartons come to the printer, not the other way round.

**Plain paper cartons and bags only, no barcodes.** An [ink roller coder](/category/ink-wheel-coders/) is a cheaper, simpler option for text-only dates on paper. The LS-1100 at $194.05 and the HP-280 at $232.90 print on cartons and paper bags with a solid ink roll, and the automatic MY-380F at $267.32 adds paging for stacks of flat cartons. But ink rollers print fixed text with type characters — if you need a barcode or QR code as well, you need inkjet.

## Step 2: Pick the right ink for your carton

Cartons are not all the same surface, and the ink has to match it.

- **Plain corrugated cardboard and kraft paper** are porous: the fibres absorb ink, so a water-based TIJ ink works well and dries into the surface.
- **Coated or glossy cartons** are non-porous: water-based ink beads up and smears. Use a solvent quick-dry ink, which dries in seconds on coated board, plastic-coated cartons and film.
- **Waxed or very smooth cartons** need a solvent ink too — and a quick adhesion test before you commit (see step 5).

Our TIJ printers use 42 ml cartridges in 12.7 mm and 25.4 mm widths, with water-based, solvent quick-dry and oil-based inks. A 12.7 mm cartridge costs from $30 and a 25.4 mm cartridge from $50. Every inkjet printer we sell includes one cartridge per print head, so you can print from day one — just make sure the cartridge you order matches your carton surface.

## Step 3: Get the print size and content right

One 12.7 mm cartridge prints one line up to 12.7 mm high — enough for a date and a batch code in one line. If you need two lines (for example, "BEST BEFORE 12/2027" plus a lot number), use a two-head printer or a 25.4 mm cartridge. Multi-head models print up to 100 mm high for large marks.

Keep the message short. An expiry date code usually needs three things: the date, the batch or lot number, and sometimes a production line code. More text means smaller characters, and smaller characters are harder to read — and harder for a scanner to read if you add a barcode. TIJ prints at 300–600 dpi, which is sharp enough for small text and scannable barcodes, but only if you give the characters room.

Use an unambiguous date format. "12/2027" is clear; "12/27" can be read as a day or a month depending on the market. If you export, check what your buyer's country expects — and keep the same format on every carton.

## Step 4: Put the code where it can be seen — and read

Print on a flat, smooth panel of the carton, not over a flap edge, tape or a crease. The printhead sits only 2–5 mm from the surface, so a bump or a fold in the board will blur the print.

Pick a spot that survives handling: the side panel is usually better than the top, which gets stacked, taped and scuffed. Leave a clear zone around the code — no tape, no labels and no printed artwork underneath it, or the date disappears into the background. If the carton is dark, the contrast matters more than the font: black ink on kraft board reads well; black ink on a dark printed panel does not.

On a conveyor, fix the print position with a guide rail or a sensor so every carton is coded in the same place. A code that wanders from carton to carton looks careless, and it slows down anyone checking the dates.

## Step 5: Test before you commit

Before you buy a machine — or run a full shift with a new one — test it on your actual cartons. Print a sample on the exact board you ship, not a clean offcut: coatings and recycled content change how ink behaves. Rub it after 10 seconds — if it smears, the ink is wrong for the surface, so switch to solvent quick-dry. Then stack and tape over it, read it from a metre away, and scan it if it includes a barcode. A code that looks fine but does not scan is worse than no code at all.

If the test fails, change the ink first — it is the cheapest fix. Changing the machine is the last resort.
