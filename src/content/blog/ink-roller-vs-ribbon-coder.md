If you code dates and batch numbers on paper bags and labels, you will meet two machines again and again: the ink roller coder (also called an ink wheel coder) and the hot ribbon coder. Both are cheap, proven and widely used — and both print perfectly well on paper. The difference is in how they print, what they cost to run, and which materials each one handles. Choose the wrong one and you get smudged codes, wasted consumables or a machine that cannot keep up with your packing table. This guide compares them head to head.

## How each one prints

**Ink roller coder.** A solid ink roll — a cylinder of solid ink — is heated on the machine until the surface is soft, then a set of movable type characters presses through it onto the bag or label. The print is instantly dry: the ink cools and sets the moment it touches the paper, so there is no smearing and nothing to wait for. The [MY-380F automatic ink roller coder](/products/my-380f/) works this way, with automatic paging and counting built in; the [HP-280](/products/hp-280/) and the [LS-1100](/products/ls-1100/) are the simpler manual and semi-automatic versions of the same idea.

**Ribbon coder.** A heated type head presses a hot-stamping ribbon against the material, transferring ink from the ribbon onto the surface. The ribbon advances a little with every print, so each impression uses a fresh section. The [HP-241 ribbon coder](/products/hp-241/) is the packaging-line version, and the [HP-241B](/products/hp-241b/) is the standalone machine for a packing station. The ribbon comes in 100 m rolls, 25, 30 or 35 mm wide, in black as standard.

Both use movable type characters, so changing the date or batch number means swapping small metal type pieces in the character holder — a few minutes of work, no software involved. Neither prints barcodes or QR codes; for variable codes like that you need a [TIJ inkjet printer](/category/inkjet-packaging-line/) instead.

## Paper bags and labels: which prints better?

On plain paper, both technologies print cleanly. The differences show up at the edges:

- **Paper bags.** The ink roller has a small advantage here. The heated ink roll presses firmly and evenly, and the ink sets instantly on porous paper, so even a slightly rough or recycled bag prints well. The MY-380F is built for exactly this job — its automatic paging feeds one bag at a time and prints as it goes, which is why its listed applications include paper bags, medicine and food packaging.
- **Paper labels.** Both print well. The ribbon coder gives a very crisp, slightly glossier impression that suits small printed labels, and the ribbon's sharp transfer shows fine characters clearly. The ink roller is equally readable but has a slightly softer, matte finish.
- **Plastic labels and film bags.** The ribbon coder wins. Hot-stamping ribbon is made for film, foil and coated surfaces — it transfers cleanly onto materials where a solid ink roll can struggle to grip. The ink roller is best kept to paper and uncoated labels.
- **Foil pouches and sachets.** Ribbon, clearly. Ribbon coding was designed for foil and film; ink roller coding on foil is possible but the adhesion is weaker.

**Rule of thumb:** if everything you code is paper, either machine works. If any of it is film, foil or plastic, choose the ribbon coder.

## Cost per print

Both machines are cheap to run, but the maths works differently.

A [solid ink roll](/products/solid-ink-roll/) costs from $0.50 per piece and fits the MY-380F, HP-280 and LS-1100. One roll prints a large number of impressions, and when it is used up you swap in a new one in seconds. There is no waste between prints: the roll only gives up ink where the type touches it.

A roll of [hot-stamping ribbon](/products/hot-stamping-ribbon/) costs from $0.80 per 100 m roll, and it fits the DY-8B, HP-241, HP-241B, HP-241C, HP-241Q and HP-501. The ribbon advances with every print, so the cost scales with message length — a short message uses little ribbon, a long one uses more. Neither machine needs ink cartridges, solvents or cleaning fluid, and neither has a print head to replace, so over a year of steady use the consumable cost of either machine is a small fraction of the machine price.

## Speed and automation

This is where the two families split most clearly.

The ink roller family covers the widest range of automation. The MY-380F ($267.32) is fully automatic: it pages bags itself, counts them, and prints up to 300 times per minute, which suits a packing line or a busy packing table. The HP-280 ($232.90) and LS-1100 ($194.05) are simpler machines for lower volumes or for fitting onto an existing packaging machine. All three print at a constant temperature, so the print quality stays even over a long run.

The ribbon family is split by use, not by speed. The HP-241 ($164.79), HP-241C and HP-241Q are packaging-line machines, designed to bolt onto a bagging or filling machine and print as the film moves through. The HP-241B ($113.05) and the DY-8B ($47.28) are standalone machines for a packing station, worked by hand or by foot pedal. Our [hot ribbon coder vs inkjet comparison](/blog/hot-ribbon-coder-vs-inkjet-printer/) works through the running costs of ribbon against inkjet.

If your volume is the deciding factor: for hand-fed paper bags at a packing table, the MY-380F's automatic paging saves the most labour. For film and foil on a packaging machine, a line ribbon coder is the natural fit.

## Which one should you buy?

- **All paper bags and paper labels, by hand, medium volume:** the [MY-380F](/products/my-380f/) — automatic paging, instant-dry print, and the lowest labour per bag.
- **Paper bags and labels, low volume, tight budget:** the [LS-1100](/products/ls-1100/) or [HP-280](/products/hp-280/) — the same ink roller printing at a lower price.
- **Film bags, foil pouches or plastic labels:** a ribbon coder — the [HP-241](/products/hp-241/) for a packaging line or the [HP-241B](/products/hp-241b/) for a packing station.
- **A mix of paper and film:** the ribbon coder covers both, and the ribbon is cheap enough that running it on paper costs almost nothing extra.
- **Barcodes, QR codes or a different message on every bag:** neither — you need a TIJ inkjet printer, which prints variable data. Our [TIJ vs CIJ guide](/blog/tij-vs-cij-inkjet-printer/) explains the options.

## A note on changing the message

Both machines use movable type, and both need the same discipline: keep a second set of type characters ready, and change the date at the start of the shift, not in the middle of a run. The copper type characters for ribbon coders and the T/R type characters for ink roller machines are cheap, and a spare set costs less than one stopped hour. Check the date on the first print of every run — a wrong date printed on a thousand bags is the most expensive mistake either machine can make.

**Bottom line:** for paper bags and labels, the ink roller coder is the faster, more automatic choice, and the MY-380F is the pick if you page bags by hand. For film, foil and plastic labels, the ribbon coder is the right tool, and the HP-241 family covers both line and standalone use. If your packaging is all paper, buy on volume and automation; if any of it is film, buy the ribbon coder.
