Pharmaceutical packaging has the least forgiving coding job in the factory. Every carton, blister pack, bottle and vial needs a batch number and an expiry date that stays readable until the medicine is used — sometimes years after it leaves the line. A smudged date on a biscuit bag is a quality complaint; a missing or unreadable batch code on a medicine carton can mean a whole shipment held at the border. Choosing the right coding machine for pharma packaging is therefore less about speed and more about legibility, traceability and clean operation. This guide explains what to look for.

## What pharma coding has to do

Before comparing machines, be clear about the job. A pharma coding machine usually has to do most of the following:

- **Print batch (lot) numbers and expiry dates** on every unit, with the date changing every run and the batch changing every production lot.
- **Print clearly at small sizes.** A medicine carton or a 10 ml vial has very little space, so characters are small — and they still have to be sharp enough to read by eye and to scan.
- **Print variable data, including barcodes and QR codes.** Serialisation and traceability rules in many markets now require a unique code on each saleable unit. That means the printer must handle changing data, not just a fixed stamp.
- **Work on many surfaces.** One factory may code paper cartons, blister foil, glass vials, plastic bottles and labels — often in the same week.
- **Run clean.** No solvent smell, no ink mist, no contamination risk near the product.

The machine that does all of this well is usually a thermal inkjet (TIJ) printer — but the right TIJ model depends on your volume and your line.

## Handheld TIJ for small batches and contract packing

If you pack in small batches, change products often, or code by hand at a packing table, a handheld TIJ printer is the cheapest way to start. It prints dates, batch numbers, barcodes and QR codes directly on cartons, bottles and blister packs, and the message is edited on a touchscreen in seconds when the batch changes.

## Online TIJ for production lines

On a packaging line, the printer sits over the conveyor and codes each pack as it passes, triggered by a sensor. The message position is identical on every unit, the speed matches the line, and the operator only touches the printer to change the batch or swap a cartridge.

## Matching the ink to the surface

The most common pharma coding failure is not the printer — it is the wrong ink for the surface. The rule is simple, and it is worth testing on your real packaging before you order:

- **Paper cartons and labels:** water-based ink. It soaks into porous surfaces and stays. It is the cheapest ink and the right choice for most outer cartons.
- **Blister foil, glass vials, plastic bottles and coated cartons:** solvent quick-dry ink. Water-based ink will not grip these surfaces; solvent ink dries in seconds and resists rubbing.
- **Dark surfaces:** white ink, so the code is visible on dark glass or coloured plastic.

Test every surface the same way: print your real message, let it dry for a few seconds, then rub it with a dry thumb and a damp cloth. If it smudges or wipes off, change the ink, not the printer. ## Ribbon coders still have a place

TIJ is not the only option. For foil pouches, sachets and flexible film — common in pharma secondary packaging — a hot ribbon coder prints sharp, smudge-proof codes by pressing heated type through a ribbon. The [HP-241 ribbon coder](/products/hp-241/) is built for packaging lines and handles exactly this work, and the [HP-241B](/products/hp-241b/) does the same job as a standalone machine at a packing station. Ribbon coding is a fixed-message technology: you change the copper type characters when the batch changes, which takes a few minutes. That makes it a poor fit for serialised, one-unit-one-code work, but an excellent, low-cost fit for batch and expiry coding on film and foil. ## What to check before you buy

Use this checklist when you talk to a supplier — it is also what we ask for when you [send us a quote request](/#quote):

1. **What exactly do you print, and on what?** List every surface: carton, foil, glass, plastic, label. One printer can cover them all only if the ink matches each surface.
2. **How big is the message?** Measure the tallest mark. 12.7 mm covers most date and batch codes; QR codes and two-line messages may need 25.4 mm or a two-head printer.
3. **Does the code change per unit?** Serialisation needs a printer that takes variable data from a file or a database, not a fixed stamp.
4. **What is your daily volume?** Under a few thousand units, handheld is fine; above that, an online printer pays for itself in consistency alone.
5. **What do your local rules require?** Traceability and serialisation requirements differ by market. The printer produces the code; confirm with your regulatory contact which code format and data your market requires, and make sure the printer you choose can print it.
6. **Can you test it first?** Send the supplier a sample of your real packaging and your real message. A five-minute print test answers more than a datasheet.

## A practical starting setup

For most small and mid-size pharma packers, the sensible starting point is a handheld TIJ printer with solvent quick-dry ink for the non-porous packs and water-based ink for cartons, plus one spare cartridge of each. That covers cartons, bottles, vials and blister packs, prints QR codes and barcodes as well as dates, and costs far less than a line installation. When volume grows, add an online printer on the conveyor and keep the handheld for short runs and rework.
