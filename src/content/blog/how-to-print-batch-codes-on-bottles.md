Bottles are one of the hardest things to code. The surface is curved, the printable area is small, glass and plastic do not absorb ink, and bottles fresh off a filling line are often damp. A printer that works beautifully on a flat carton can smear, skip or miss entirely on a bottle. This guide explains the three ways to put a batch code on a bottle — inkjet, ribbon and laser — and which one fits your bottles, your volume and your budget.

## Why bottles are difficult

Three things make bottle coding different from carton coding:

1. **The curve.** A bottle is round, so only a narrow strip of it is flat enough to print on. The print head has to sit 2–5 mm from the surface, and on a curved bottle that distance changes across the width of the print. Print too wide a message and the edges blur.
2. **The surface.** Glass and most plastics are non-porous. Water-based ink sits on top and wipes off. Only solvent quick-dry ink — or a permanent mark — stays put.
3. **The size.** A bottle's flat area is small. A date and batch code has to fit in one short line, often 12.7 mm high or less, and it has to be readable after the bottle is handled, chilled or shipped.

Get those three right and bottle coding is straightforward. Get them wrong and you get smeared codes, failed inspections or returned stock.

## Inkjet (TIJ): the best choice for most bottlers

A thermal inkjet (TIJ) printer is the most practical way to code bottles for small and medium volumes. It prints dates, batch codes, barcodes and QR codes in one pass, it needs no contact with the bottle beyond a 2–5 mm gap, and with the right ink it prints on glass, PET, HDPE, metal and plastic.

**The ink is the decision.** For bottles, use solvent quick-dry ink. It dries in seconds on glass and plastic, where water-based ink would smear or wipe off. Water-based ink is for paper and cardboard only — never for bottles. Our 42 ml TIJ cartridges come in both ink types, in black, white, red, blue, yellow and green; white ink is worth considering for dark glass or black plastic, where black ink disappears. ## Ribbon coders: right for bags, wrong for bottles

A hot ribbon coder — the [DY-8B](/products/dy-8b/) and the HP-241 family — prints by pressing a heated type head through an ink ribbon onto the surface. It is cheap to run (a ribbon roll costs $0.80) and the print is crisp. But it only works on flexible film and foil: the ribbon has to press flat against the material, and the material has to give a little under the head.

A rigid glass or plastic bottle does neither. The head cannot follow the curve, the ribbon cannot press evenly, and the print comes out patchy or blank. Ribbon coders are excellent for the bags and pouches that bottles are packed in — but for the bottle itself, they are the wrong tool. Our comparison, [hot ribbon coder vs inkjet printer](/blog/hot-ribbon-coder-vs-inkjet-printer/), explains the cost and quality difference in detail.

There is one exception worth knowing: if you code the bottle's label rather than the bottle, a ribbon coder can work — but only if the label is printed before it is applied to the bottle, while it is still flat film. Once the label is on the bottle, it is a bottle-coding job again.

## Laser: permanent, but a different budget

A laser coder burns the code into the surface — no ink, no consumables, and the mark cannot be rubbed off. For glass and some plastics it gives a clean, permanent code, and for very high-volume lines running day and night it can be the cheapest option per print over years.

But laser is a different purchase. A laser coder costs many times more than a TIJ printer, it needs fume extraction, and it needs an operator who understands laser safety and marking parameters for each material. For most small and medium bottlers — a few thousand bottles a day, a few product changes a week — that investment does not pay back. We do not sell laser coders; if your volume genuinely justifies one, get a quote from a laser supplier and compare it against a TIJ setup on total cost, not on the machine price alone.

## Five practical tips for clean bottle codes

1. **Print on the flattest area.** The label panel or the shoulder, not the neck or the base curve. The flatter the surface, the sharper the print.
2. **Keep the head 2–5 mm from the surface.** Too far and the ink sprays; too close and the head can touch the bottle. On a conveyor, a simple guide rail keeps every bottle at the same distance.
3. **Dry the bottle first.** Condensation is the enemy of any ink. If bottles come off a cold filling line, let them reach room temperature or wipe the print area before coding.
4. **Test adhesion before you commit.** Print your real message on your real bottle, let it dry, then rub it with a dry thumb and a damp cloth. If it smudges, you have the wrong ink — switch to solvent quick-dry before you code a production run.
5. **Keep the message short.** Date, batch code, and nothing else unless you need a barcode or QR code. Short messages print sharper on a curve and use far less ink per bottle, which is what decides your cost per print.
